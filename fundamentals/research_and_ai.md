# 🔍 Research and AI

### Find the real answer, catch the confident fake, and use AI without handing it your brain

> **Grades:** 7th to 12th &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Time:** 2 class periods &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Difficulty:** Beginner
>
> The internet has the answer to almost anything you will ever need to know. It also has a confident, beautifully written, completely wrong answer to the same question, usually higher up the page. Over two days you will learn to search like someone who knows what they are doing, work out whether a source has earned your trust, and use AI in the way that makes you sharper instead of the way that leaves you standing in front of a class unable to explain your own work.

---

## 🎯 What You'll Learn

- How to search with **operators** instead of typing whole questions
- How to run the **SIFT** check on a source before you trust it
- How to tell a **primary source** from someone's summary of one
- How to use AI to understand something, and where that crosses into cheating
- How to catch a **hallucination**, which is AI stating something false with total confidence

---

## 🧰 What You Need

- A computer with a web browser
- An AI tool you are old enough to use. **Read the age table in Day 2 before you pick one.**
- Something you are actually stuck on from another class, for the Day 2 activity

---

# Day 1: Find It and Check It

**Goal:** By the end of today you can find a specific fact fast, and you can say out loud why you do or do not trust the page you found it on.

---

### Step 1: Stop typing questions

Most people type the entire question into the search bar, politely, as though the search engine might be offended otherwise.

```
What is the best programming language to learn to get a job in 2026?
```

That works, and it is slow, and the search engine throws away most of those words before it does anything. Here are five operators that do the real work.

| Operator | What you type | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Keywords | `best programming language jobs 2026` | Says the same thing with nothing wasted |
| Quotes | `"entry level" software developer salary` | Demands that exact phrase, in that order |
| Minus | `python tutorial -youtube` | Removes results containing a word |
| `site:` | `site:nhc.noaa.gov st croix` | Searches inside one website only |
| `filetype:` | `raspberry pi projects filetype:pdf` | Finds one kind of file |

`site:` is the one that changes how you work. It lets you search a source you already trust instead of searching the whole internet and hoping.

1. Try each of the five on a question of your own
2. Notice which one gave you a usable answer fastest

> **✅ Checkpoint:** You have run all five operators and can say what each one did to your results.

---

### Step 2: The Search Challenge

Three questions. For each one, write down **the exact query you typed** and **the source you landed on**. The query matters as much as the answer.

1. Find a beginner Raspberry Pi project guide as a PDF. Use `filetype:`.
2. Find what the National Hurricane Center itself published about a storm that affected St. Croix. Use `site:nhc.noaa.gov`.
3. Find the salary range for a job you would actually want, in the Virgin Islands. Use quotes around the exact phrase.

Question 3 is harder than it looks. Territory specific salary data is thin, and you may not find a clean number.

> **If you cannot find it, write "I could not find this" and say where you looked.** That is a real research result. Inventing a number, or grabbing a US national average and calling it a Virgin Islands number, is how bad information gets made.

> **✅ Checkpoint:** Three queries written down, three sources named, and an honest answer on any you could not find.

---

### Step 3: Run SIFT before you trust it

Four moves, in order, before you believe anything.

| Letter | Move | What you actually do |
|---|---|---|
| **S** | Stop | Do not share it yet. Do not screenshot it yet. Pause. |
| **I** | Investigate the source | Who wrote this. What is this site. Are they selling something. |
| **F** | Find better coverage | Search the same claim. Does anyone credible say it too. |
| **T** | Trace to the original | Articles cite studies. Go read the study. Summaries get it wrong constantly. |

**T** is the one everybody skips, and it is where the errors are hiding. An article says "a study found teenagers who code earn more." You trace it back and the study surveyed 200 adults in one city. Nobody technically lied. The claim just quietly grew on its way to the headline.

**Red flags that should slow you down**

| Red flag | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| No author | "Staff Writer", or nothing at all |
| No date | You cannot tell if this is from last month or 2014 |
| Emotional headline | "SHOCKING", "You Won't Believe" |
| Ads outnumber content | Low effort site making money on clicks |
| Nothing cited | Big claims, no links to evidence |
| Only one site says it | Nobody credible repeated it |

**How much a source is worth**

| Tier | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Best | The original thing | Published studies, government data, official documentation |
| Good | Established outlets and schools | AP, Reuters, BBC, `.edu` sites, MDN Web Docs |
| Okay as a starting point | Community checked | Stack Overflow answers with high votes, Wikipedia, then follow its sources |
| Careful | One person's take | Blog posts, videos, social media. Sometimes excellent, sometimes invented. |
| Avoid | Made for clicks | No author, filler articles, sites that exist to rank on search |

**Do this:** find one source you trust and one you do not, on any tech topic. Run SIFT on both. Screenshot both and write which SIFT move gave the bad one away.

> **✅ Checkpoint:** Two sources, two SIFT verdicts, and you can name the specific move that exposed the weak one.

---

### Step 2: Where the line actually is

Same assignment, two ways of using the same tool.

**This is cheating:**
```
Write my essay about the American Revolution.
```
You learned nothing, you handed in work that is not yours, and you cannot survive one follow up question. Mr. Krigger asks follow up questions.

**This is learning:**
```
I am writing an essay about the American Revolution. Explain the main causes
simply so I understand them before I write anything.
```
You learned the material, the writing is yours, and you can defend it.

The test is one question: **am I using this to understand, or to avoid understanding?**

The class rule follows from that. You may use AI to explain things, to help you debug, and to write code when Mr. Krigger approves it or the assignment asks for it. What you submit has to be yours, and you have to be able to explain any part of it on demand.

---

### Step 3: Prompt patterns worth keeping

These are the ones that actually help you learn. Try at least two on something you are stuck on right now, from any class.

**Explain it at my level**
```
Explain how CSS Flexbox works to an 8th grader. Use one real example.
```

**Quiz me, and do not give it away**
```
Give me 5 practice questions on Python loops. Do not show any answers
until I have tried each one.
```

**Check my wrong idea**
```
I think a for loop in Python always runs exactly 10 times. Am I right?
If not, explain why.
```

**Argue both sides**
```
What are the arguments for and against using AI in schools?
Give both sides fairly.
```

**Explain why, not just what**
```
Walk me through setting up a Raspberry Pi web server. For every step,
explain why the step is there.
```

That last pattern is worth more than the other four combined. Instructions you can follow without understanding are instructions you will need again next week, and the week after.

> **✅ Checkpoint:** Two of these patterns run on something real, and you understand something you did not understand at the start of the period.

### 🧠 Mini Lesson: why a confident answer can still be invented

An AI is not looking anything up. It is predicting what text should come next, one piece at a time, from patterns in an enormous pile of writing it was trained on. It is an extremely well read autocomplete.

That is why it writes so well and why it can be so wrong. Sounding right and being right come out of the same machinery, so nothing inside it flags which one just happened. When it does not know something it does not stop, or hesitate, or go quiet. It produces the most plausible looking text available, which is unfortunately what a correct answer also looks like.

This is called a **hallucination**. It will cite studies that were never published, quote people who never said it, and give you a street address for a business that does not exist, all in the same confident tone it uses for things that are true.

Three other limits worth knowing. It has a **knowledge cutoff**, a date its training stopped, so it can be confidently out of date on prices, versions, and current events. It reflects the **bias** in what it was trained on. And if you let it answer everything for you, your own ability quietly stops developing, which is the slowest and most expensive problem on this list.

The fix for all four is the same, and you learned it yesterday. Trace it back to the original.

---

### Step 4: Catch one

**Base tier, everyone does this.** Ask your AI tool something specific and local, where you can check the answer yourself:

- The address or opening hours of a specific business on St. Croix
- Who currently holds a specific local office
- What a particular store charges for something

Then verify it against the real source. Write down what it said, what is actually true, and how you checked.

**Stretch tier, required for high school, bonus for middle school.** Make it cite something, then chase the citation.

1. Ask a factual question and add: `Cite your sources with links.`
2. Take every citation it gives you and try to open it
3. For each one, record: does the page exist, does it say what the AI claimed, is it a real source

Report what you found. Sometimes every link is real. Sometimes a link works but does not contain the claim, which is the most dangerous case, because it looks verified until you actually read it.

> **✅ Checkpoint:** You have one documented case of the AI being wrong, out of date, or citing something that does not support its claim. If everything checked out, say so and show your checking.

## 🧠 Vocabulary to Know

| Term | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Operator** | A search command like `site:` or `filetype:` that narrows results instead of adding words |
| **SIFT** | Stop, Investigate, Find better coverage, Trace to the original |
| **Primary Source** | The original: the study itself, the official data, the person who was there |
| **Secondary Source** | Somebody's account of a primary source, like a news article about a study |
| **Hallucination** | AI stating something false in exactly the tone it uses for things that are true |
| **Knowledge Cutoff** | The date an AI's training stopped. Anything after it, the model is guessing with total confidence. |
| **Bias** | A slant an AI inherits from the writing it was trained on |
| **Prompt** | What you type to an AI tool |
| **Content Farm** | A site that exists to collect ad clicks, with articles as the packaging |
| **Paywall** | A page you have to pay to read. A genuine obstacle, and still not a reason to fall back on a worse source |

## 💡 Troubleshooting

**"My `site:` search returns nothing."**
→ Check the domain is spelled exactly right and drop the `https://` and `www.`. It should read `site:nhc.noaa.gov`, with no space after the colon.

**"I cannot find the Virgin Islands salary number."**
→ Good. That is the honest answer for a lot of territory specific data, and finding out that something is not published is a real result. Write down where you looked.

**"The AI gave me a source but the link is dead."**
→ Write it down immediately, because that is exactly what this assignment is hunting for. Then search the title on its own and find out whether the source ever existed at all.

**"I am under 18, so what do I use?"**
→ Microsoft Copilot. The school pays for it and the minimum age is 13. If you cannot get in, come find Mr. Krigger rather than quietly borrowing someone's account.

**"The article I need is behind a paywall."**
→ Check whether your library gives you access, look for the original study the article is describing, or note the paywall and use a different source. Do not pay for anything for this class.

**"Everything the AI told me checked out."**
→ Then say so, and show your working. Failing to find a mistake is a real result when you genuinely looked for one. It is not a real result when you did not.
